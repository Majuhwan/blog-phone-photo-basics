Phone Camera Settings Worth Checking Today

A rundown of iPhone camera modes and controls worth knowing, based directly on official Apple Support documentation.

Phone Photo BasicsOct 9, 2026 · 3 min read

Most people use the same one or two camera settings on their phone forever, simply because they never scrolled through what else is available. Apple's own support documentation for the iPhone camera lists several modes and controls that are easy to miss if you've only ever tapped the shutter button in the default Photo mode.

The camera modes you can swipe to

Apple's iPhone camera basics guide lists the available modes you can switch to by swiping left or right on the camera screen, depending on your specific iPhone model:

  • Cinematic: applies a depth-of-field effect to videos.
  • Portrait: applies a depth-of-field effect to photos (the blurred-background look).
  • Spatial: captures spatial photos and videos meant to be viewed in 3D on Apple Vision Pro.
  • Pano: captures a panoramic landscape or wide scene.
  • Reference (on iPhone 18 Pro models): captures a reference image alongside your photo so you can later verify whether the photo has been edited or altered.

Most people default to Photo mode because it's what the app opens to. If you regularly photograph people up close, Portrait mode is worth testing instead of relying on Photo mode followed by editing later.

Setting a different default mode

According to Apple's documentation, Camera opens to Photo mode by default, but you can change this. Apple's guide references a separate setting ("Save camera settings on iPhone") specifically for this, meaning if you shoot in Pano or Portrait mode often, you don't have to manually switch modes every time you open the camera.

Camera Controls button

Apple's documentation describes a dedicated Camera Controls button available when Camera is open, which lets you "quickly make adjustments to features like Flash or Exposure" by tapping the feature you want to adjust and changing its settings directly, without digging through a separate settings menu. On supported models, lightly double-pressing the physical Camera Control button opens an overlay showing available settings, including Zoom, which you then adjust by sliding your finger.

Night mode: when it turns on and what to do

Apple's support page on taking great photos states that Night mode "automatically improves photos taken in low light on supported iPhone models," and you can tell it's active because the Night Mode button appears when it's on. The single most actionable instruction in Apple's own documentation here: "Tap the Shutter button, then hold your iPhone steady for the best results." Night mode photos take longer to capture than a normal photo, since the camera is collecting more light over a longer exposure, so movement during that window is the most common reason a Night mode photo comes out blurry.

Apple's dedicated Night mode guide adds a specific troubleshooting detail: if crosshairs appear in the frame during capture, the iPhone has detected movement, and aligning them helps reduce motion and improve the final image quality. For best results in Night mode specifically, Apple's documentation also recommends using a tripod.

Editing without leaving the Photos app

After taking a photo, Apple's documentation describes a direct editing flow: open the photo or video, tap Edit, then use the buttons provided to adjust lighting, add a filter, crop, or rotate. While editing, tapping the photo lets you compare your edited version against the original directly, which is useful for checking whether an edit (particularly exposure or filter changes) has gone too far compared to the original capture.

A simple checklist to run through once

  1. Check your default mode. If you shoot mostly portraits or panoramas, consider changing your default from Photo mode.
  2. Learn the Camera Controls button location on your specific model. It gives direct access to Flash and Exposure without a settings menu.
  3. Watch for the Night Mode indicator in low light, and remember to hold steady, since the exposure window is longer than a normal photo.
  4. Use the Edit screen's compare-to-original feature when adjusting lighting or filters, so you can tell whether you've actually improved the photo or just changed it.

Key takeaways

  • iPhone camera modes beyond Photo (Portrait, Pano, Cinematic, Spatial, and Reference on newer Pro models) are available by swiping, and you can change which one opens by default.
  • The Camera Controls button gives direct, in-camera access to Flash and Exposure adjustments.
  • Night mode activates automatically in low light; holding the phone steady matters more here than in normal lighting because the exposure takes longer, and crosshairs in the frame indicate detected motion.
  • The Photos app's Edit screen lets you tap the photo to compare your edit against the unedited original directly.

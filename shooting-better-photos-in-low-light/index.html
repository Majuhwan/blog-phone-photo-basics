Shooting Better Photos in Low Light

What Apple and Google officially document about taking low-light and night photos on iPhone and Pixel phones, without special equipment.

Phone Photo BasicsOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Low light is the single hardest everyday condition for a phone camera, since the sensor needs to gather more light than normal, which takes time and makes movement far more visible in the final result. Both Apple and Google document specific features built to handle this, and both documentation sets agree on the same underlying piece of advice: stability matters more here than almost anything else.

What's actually happening in low light

Google's own explanation, published alongside the launch of its Night Sight feature, describes the core problem plainly: "When it's dark, cameras compensate by spending more time capturing light and increasing their sensitivity to light, often adding unwanted motion blur and noise to your photo." This is the reason low-light photos taken casually, without any adjustment, tend to come out either blurry (from the longer exposure catching hand movement) or grainy (from the camera boosting sensitivity to compensate for the dark scene).

The iPhone approach: Night mode

Apple's support documentation describes Night mode as a feature that "automatically brightens photos and captures more detail in low light" on supported iPhone models. It turns on automatically; Apple's guide states plainly that "in low-light situations, Night mode turns on automatically," and you can toggle it manually with the Night mode button if you prefer it off.

Apple's documentation gives one specific manual adjustment option: tapping the Camera Controls button, then Night Mode, lets you choose between available exposure options, and your choice is saved for the next Night mode photo you take. A supported-model tip from the same documentation: "Use a tripod for even more detailed Night mode photos," since a tripod removes the hand-movement variable entirely during the longer exposure window.

The most concrete troubleshooting detail in Apple's Night mode guide: "If crosshairs appear in the frame, your iPhone has detected movement, align them to help you reduce motion and improve the image quality." This is a direct, real-time signal from the phone itself telling you it's detecting shake during the shot, and the fix according to Apple's own instructions is to physically align the displayed crosshairs, not just "try to hold stiller" without guidance.

The Pixel approach: Night Sight

Google's Pixel Camera Help documentation describes a similar but separately named feature, Night Sight, for taking photos "in low light or of the night sky without the flash." Google's support page breaks its advice into two categories: preparing for outdoor photography, and adjusting the device's settings, specifically calling out brightness and shadow adjustment as controls worth using after the photo, not just before.

For devices from Pixel 4a 5G and later, Google's documentation describes a specific editing path: tap Edit, then Adjust, to reach separate sliders for Brightness and Shadows. This mirrors Apple's general editing flow in spirit (adjust after capture, compare against the result) but is specific to the Pixel Camera app's own Night Sight-captured images.

Why flash usually isn't the answer

Google's explanation of why Night Sight exists at all is also a direct explanation of why flash is often the wrong fix for low light: "Flash helps by adding light to the scene, but it awkwardly blinds your friends and can annoy others around you... Flash also only brightens nearby subjects and isn't useful for landscapes or faraway objects." If you're trying to photograph a dim room, a cityscape at night, or the sky, flash light reaches at most a few feet, so it does nothing for anything farther away, which is most of what's actually in a low-light or night photo.

A short low-light checklist based on both documents

  1. Let the automatic low-light mode trigger (Night mode on iPhone, Night Sight on Pixel); both are designed to turn on without you needing to dig through settings.
  2. Hold the phone as still as possible for the full capture, since both platforms' documentation treats motion during the longer exposure as the main cause of a blurry low-light shot. If your iPhone shows crosshairs, align them before the capture finishes.
  3. Use a tripod when the scene allows it, especially for the sky or distant landscapes, since Apple's documentation specifically recommends this for the most detailed Night mode results.
  4. Skip the flash for anything more than a few feet away; it won't reach distant subjects and will only brighten whatever is directly in front of the lens.
  5. Adjust brightness and shadows after the photo is taken, using the editing tools both platforms document, rather than trying to get the exposure perfect in a single shot.

Key takeaways

  • Both iPhone's Night mode and Pixel's Night Sight work by extending the exposure time, which is why holding the phone steady matters more in low light than in any other common shooting condition.
  • Apple's Night mode shows crosshairs in real time when it detects movement; aligning them is the documented way to reduce blur.
  • A tripod is specifically recommended by Apple's own documentation for the most detailed low-light results.
  • Flash is not an effective general fix for low light, since it only brightens subjects within a few feet and does nothing for landscapes, night skies, or distant scenes.
  • Both platforms document post-capture brightness and shadow adjustment tools, meaning you don't need to nail the exposure perfectly in the original low-light shot.
